Abstract

Dynamic manipulation places two distinct information requirements on a visuomotor policy. Motion must be observable: the policy has to see how the scene is changing, not only its current appearance. And motion must be calibrated: displacement scale and elapsed time have to survive all the way to the policy, so that slow and fast versions of the same scene do not collapse into one condition. A policy conditioned only on the current observation is dynamically aliased — two trajectories can reach the same image and robot state while the target moves at different velocities. Rendered optical flow makes motion visible, but per-map normalization makes the rendering scale-invariant: it preserves direction and spatial structure while discarding globally comparable speed.

DynamicWAM conditions a compact video–action model through two complementary paths. Four rendered head-camera flow maps expose where motion occurs and its spatial structure; four exact-time 12-dimensional motion descriptors — displacement, interval duration, velocity, acceleration — are mapped to action-side motion tokens that restore what the rendering removed. The two paths meet through layer-wise joint attention between a compact video expert and an action expert. Under the DOMINO Level-1 native synchronous protocol (35 tasks × 100 episodes, execute-16), the motion-free Efficient-WAM reference obtains 22.7% success, History-Flow WAM 27.2%, and the complete DynamicWAM pipeline reaches 38.2% success with a manipulation score of 53.2.

The problem

The current frame is not enough — and normalized flow is not calibrated

A motion-free world-action model conditions on the current composite observation, the language instruction, and the robot state. In a dynamic scene this condition is ambiguous by construction: the same image and the same joint readings are compatible with a target that is creeping and a target that is sprinting. Whatever the policy does next, it is guessing the one variable that decides interception — velocity.

Adding rendered optical-flow history repairs observability, but not calibration. Each flow map is rendered with direction as hue and magnitude normalized by that map's own 99th percentile, so scaling the underlying flow field leaves the rendering unchanged:

ρ(αU) = ρ(U), α > 0

The image can say “the target is moving up-right, over here” — it cannot say how fast. DynamicWAM's answer is a division of labor, not generic multimodal fusion: flow images carry spatial motion structure, and a separate numeric path carries the magnitude and time variables the rendering discards.

Cflow = Ceff + (R1…R4) observable, uncalibrated
Cfull = Cflow + (m1…m4) observable + calibrated

The 12 numbers the rendering can't keep

Each of the four intervals contributes one exact-time descriptor, standardized with dataset-global statistics. Spatial quantities live on the 64×64 flow grid; time is exact simulator seconds. Invalid intervals get learned validity embeddings — missing evidence is never silently treated as zero motion.
