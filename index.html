DynamicWAM: Dual-Path Motion Conditioning for Dynamic Manipulation

Abstract

Dynamic manipulation requires robots to infer target motion and respond promptly, yet existing World–Action Models (WAMs) typically condition only on the current frame and execute large backbones synchronously, limiting motion awareness and responsive control in dynamic scenes. We propose DynamicWAM, a compact WAM for dynamic object manipulation with dual-path motion conditioning. DynamicWAM introduces history-flow conditioning, encoding temporally aligned optical-flow maps alongside the current observation through an unchanged pretrained video VAE to preserve spatial motion structure, while injecting numerical descriptors of displacement, duration, velocity, and acceleration into the action expert to provide metric scale and temporal dynamics.

The two complementary paths are fused through joint world–action attention. A distilled compact backbone and real-time chunking (RTC)-based asynchronous execution further enable responsive control. On DOMINO, DynamicWAM achieves a 38.2% success rate and a 53.2 manipulation score, outperforming all evaluated baselines. Across 12 real-world tasks spanning linear, circular, and complex target motion, it achieves a 46.7% average success rate, exceeding the strongest baseline by 22.9 percentage points. These results demonstrate that dual-path motion conditioning effectively enables motion-aware control of moving targets.

The Problem

A current-frame world–action model conditions on the current composite observation, the language instruction, and the robot state. In a dynamic scene this condition is ambiguous by construction: the same image and the same joint readings are compatible with a target that is creeping and a target that is sprinting. Whatever the policy does next, it is guessing the one variable that decides interception — velocity.

Adding rendered optical-flow history repairs observability, but not calibration. Each flow map is rendered with direction as hue and magnitude normalized by that map's own 99th percentile, so scaling the underlying flow field leaves the rendering unchanged:

ρ(αU) = ρ(U), α > 0

The image can say "the target is moving up-right, over here" — it cannot say how fast. DynamicWAM's answer is a division of labor, not generic multimodal fusion: flow images carry spatial motion structure, and a separate numerical path carries the magnitude and time variables the rendering discards.

Method

DynamicWAM augments a current-frame world–action model with dual-path motion conditioning. The visual path renders a short history of head-camera optical flow and encodes it together with the current observation; the numerical path injects descriptors of displacement, duration, velocity, and acceleration into the action expert. Flow preserves the spatial organization of motion — the numbers preserve its magnitude and rate.

History-flow conditioning

At each chunk boundary, the recent history is divided into K = 4 intervals whose endpoints are Δ = 4 policy steps apart. For every interval, dense Farnebäck flow from the fixed head camera passes a forward–backward consistency check; direction becomes hue and magnitude is normalized by the map's own 99th percentile. The four rendered maps are concatenated with the current observation and encoded by the unchanged pretrained video VAE — recent motion travels the same latent pathway as visual appearance, with no separate motion encoder and no change to the robot action space.

Numerical motion tokens

Each interval also contributes one 12-D descriptor — exactly the quantities that per-map normalization and rendering discard. Descriptors are standardized with dataset-level statistics, projected by a two-layer MLP, and inserted beside the state and action tokens as four motion tokens. Invalid intervals get learned validity embeddings — missing evidence is never silently treated as zero motion.

Fusion and execution

A compact video expert distilled from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B and an action expert attend to each other's keys and values in every block — joint world–action attention. The decoder reads only the state–action core and outputs a 16 × 14 joint-position chunk. In simulation, inference follows DOMINO's native synchronous protocol; on real robots, Real-Time Chunking predicts the next chunk while the current one executes.

Training

The final training set contains 10,500 episodes — 150 clean and 150 randomized demonstrations for each of the 35 tasks — yielding 1.43M training windows. Training proceeds in three stages: 80k steps of video distillation from the Wan2.2 teacher, 80k steps of action training, and 40k steps of joint fine-tuning. Inference uses 10 flow-matching steps with the video stream's keys and values cached after step 1; each predicted chunk executes 16 actions at a nominal 100 ms interval.

Simulation Results

We evaluate on DOMINO Level 1 in the clean dynamic setting: all 35 tasks, 100 accepted episodes per task, unseen language instructions, under the benchmark's native synchronous protocol. DynamicWAM outperforms every evaluated method on both success rate and manipulation score, exceeding the strongest baseline, InternVLA-A1.5, by 8.9 SR points.

Ablation: two paths, two gains

History flow answers where motion is; numerical motion tokens answer how much and how fast. Each path adds its own step:

The headline result is statistically solid: a stratified task–episode bootstrap gives a 95% confidence interval of [36.9%, 39.5%] for the 38.2% success rate. Among the 3,225 episodes where the full and w/o-motion models share the same task and raw scene seed, the paired success-rate difference is +11.2 points, with an exact two-sided McNemar p = 2.8×10−33. At the task level, the full model wins / ties / loses on 23 / 2 / 10 tasks against the w/o-motion variant, and 24 / 1 / 10 against the current-frame variant.

Task-level analysis

The largest improvements occur in tasks that require accurately timed contact with a moving target. On move_pillbottle_pad, success increases from 42% (current-frame) to 55% (+ flow) and 91% (full model); on beat_block_hammer, from 36% to 49% to 84%. The numerical path matters most where spatial structure alone is insufficient: on place_phone_stand, history flow does not help (33% → 32%), while adding numerical motion tokens lifts success to 80%. The gains are not uniform — the full model trails the flow-only variant on put_object_cabinet (−10) and place_can_basket (−8); complete per-task results are in the paper's appendix.

Real-World Results

We construct a 12-task real-robot evaluation suite in three motion regimes: linear — an RC vehicle, an inclined rail, a conveyor belt, a self-propelled toy; uniform circular — objects on a rotating turntable; and nonlinear or compound — random bouncing, simultaneous translation and rotation, curved trajectories. Every method is fine-tuned on the same 1,200 demonstrations and evaluated for 20 trials per task on Franka Research 3 arms with parallel-jaw grippers. DynamicWAM deploys with Real-Time Chunking.
